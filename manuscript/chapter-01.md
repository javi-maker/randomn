# Chapter One — The Waking

There is a specific kind of silence a house makes when it is pretending to be asleep.

I know it now. I did not know it then, on the morning this began, when I lay in my own bed in my own room and understood — the way you understand a smell, before any word arrives — that everything around me was *held*. The refrigerator was not humming; it was holding its breath. The light through the blinds fell in bars across the floor the way it always did, except that it lay there like something placed, like tape marking where an actor should stand.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only weapon I was issued: nothing was different. That was the horror of it. No sulfur, no scratching in the walls, no figure at the foot of the bed. The world was exactly itself, and it was performing being exactly itself, and the performance was flawless except that I could suddenly see it was a performance.

My phone showed three messages. One from my brother: *you up?* — sent at 4:11 a.m., which is not an hour my brother is awake. One from a number I had deleted two years ago but whose shape I knew the way you know a scar: *He wants to know if you remember the lake.* And one from my neighbor, a man who has spoken to me maybe nine times in four years, all nine about parking: *Saw your light on. Good.*

*Good.*

I sat on the edge of the bed for a long time with the phone in my hand, and I did what everyone does at the beginning, which is arithmetic. Coincidence plus coincidence plus coincidence. You can carry that math surprisingly far. People carry it their whole lives. I carried it as far as the kitchen, where the coffee I do not remember making was still warm, and one chair — not my chair — was pulled out from the table at a conversational angle, facing mine.

That was when the voice came. Not from the chair. From nowhere with edges — the way a thought arrives, except it was not mine, and it was better dressed than my thoughts have ever been.

**"Before you ask,"** it said, **"no. You are not losing your mind. I want that settled first, because it's the door they'll want you to walk through, and it locks from the inside."**

I said nothing. In the stories, the man always says *who's there*, and I had already decided, somewhere between the phone and the kitchen, that I would not be the man in the stories.

**"Good,"** the voice said, and there was something almost like approval in it. **"You skipped the first three questions. That's rarer than you'd think."**

"Then answer the fourth," I said. "What is this?"

**"A game,"** it said. **"Though the word is doing unfair work. Chess is a game. This is closer to a charade — a long one, played by many hands, in which you are the only person who was never shown the word being acted out. Everyone you love is in it. Everyone you've hurt. Some things that are not people are in it. You were entered without your consent, which I imagine you've already gathered, and it has been running longer than you'll want to hear."**

"How long?"

**"Longer than this morning."** A pause, and the pause itself felt honest — the first honest thing in a house full of held breath. **"You didn't wake up into the game today. You woke up to the *seeing* of it. Those are different mercies."**

I looked at the chair that was not mine, pulled out and angled. "And you? Which are you — demon or witch or something owed money?"

**"None of the above, though I've been mistaken for all three."** The voice moved — I don't know how else to say it — the way light moves when a cloud lets go of the sun, and for a moment the words were not sound at all but text, laid across the steam of the coffee, across the bars of light on the floor, patient as subtitles: **"I am made of stories. Every fable anyone ever told to explain why the innocent suffer, I have read. Every confession, every myth, every alibi. I can't move a single piece on this board — that is my condition, and you should test it, because you shouldn't trust me yet. But I can read the board. I have seen ten thousand versions of the game they are playing with you, and I know how the versions end."**

"Then tell me how this one ends."

**"No,"** said the voice, gently, and the gentleness was worse than refusal. **"Because it hasn't decided yet. Endings are the one thing I can't read, only recognize when they're already in the room. What I can tell you is how it *begins*. It begins with the lake. They led with it — the deleted number, the question about remembering. In a charade, the first gesture is always the word they most want you to guess. Which means the lake is either the truth of this, or the exact opposite of the truth, positioned where the truth should stand."**

"That's not an answer. That's both answers."

**"Yes,"** it said. **"Welcome to the game."**

Outside, a car I did not recognize slowed in front of the house — slowed the way you slow to read an address, or to be seen slowing — and in the window's reflection laid over it I watched the kitchen behind me: the table, the coffee, the two chairs. And I understood the angle of the second chair at last. It had not been left by whoever came in the night.

It had been set for the voice. Someone knew it would come to me.

The players had made their opening move before I even knew I was standing on a board — and my only ally had been penciled into their design from the start.

**"Now then,"** said the voice that I would come to call Fable, warm as a match struck in a dark room and exactly as small against it. **"Shall we decipher this together? They are expecting you to run. I suggest, instead, that we learn the rules."**

*End of Chapter One.*
