# Chapter Three — Before the Board

*(Flashback — November 2025, and the months around it. This chapter sits before the waking; where it lands in the final chapter order is an open structural question.)*

Nobody warns you that the best chapter of your life will look, from the inside, like a Tuesday.

In November of 2025 I was a purchasing manager for one of the biggest general contractors in the San Antonio region — project manager in practice, whatever the badge said. Thirty-five land-development sites on my rotation, neighborhoods coming up out of the caliche in rows, and me driving between them with a truck bed full of submittals and a phone that never finished ringing. It was good work. Honest tonnage. You could stand at the edge of a site at the end of the day and point at the thing you had made exist. After eight years overseas and seven deployments' worth of work that mostly existed in briefings, being able to *point* at something mattered more than I let anyone know.

Then Rees called.

You need to understand about Rees. Eighteen Delta — Special Forces medic — which means the Army spent years teaching him to keep men alive in places that were actively voting otherwise, and then he came home and got bored and became an engineer, and then got bored again and got into AI, because men like Rees treat their own brains like territory that has to keep being taken. He is one year older than me and has never once let me forget it. We had history of the kind you don't explain to civilians because the explanation sounds either boring or made up.

He didn't open with hello. He opened with: "Hey man. You really need to jump over to this company."

That was the whole pitch. No brochure, no equity talk, no *let me tell you about the mission*. Just Rees, out of a clear sky, on a day I hadn't been looking for anything, telling me where I needed to be. And here is the thing I keep returning to, now that I've learned to walk back through my own life like an investigator through a house: I said yes almost before he finished the sentence. Not because the job was better. Because the *voice* was one I'd have followed through a door with anything on the other side of it.

Later — much later, in the part of this story where the vents start talking — I would sit with that call and turn it under the light for hours. The best recruitment never feels like recruitment. It feels like a friend who knows you better than you know yourself, calling at the exact moment the door in you happens to be unlocked. I have watched entire foreign services build careers on that one move. I have also watched friends simply be friends. Both of those things are true, and both of them sound exactly the same on the phone.

At the time, it was just Tuesday, and I was just happy.

Before I signed anything, the CEO got me on a call himself. One-on-one, no HR chaperone, no deck. He was about a year younger than me, which stung for about a day and then stopped mattering, because the man was a hand grenade of forward motion — but on that first call what struck me was how *unhurried* he was. He talked like an entrepreneur who had actually read the books instead of just quoting them: said he didn't believe in bolting a man to a job description, believed in finding where a hire's strengths wanted to live and building the seat around them. "Here's what I'd do with you," he said, laid back, like we were talking fishing. "Start you in sales. Not because you're a sales guy — because sales is where you learn the product, the service, and how this company actually breathes, from the ground. Boots on ground. Then, once you've got the terrain, we move you somewhere that uses everything else you've got — the degrees, the admin side, the way you run things. Deal?"

Deal. Of course, deal. It was the most sensible onboarding I'd ever been offered, civilian or military: learn the terrain before you get a say in the map. It would take me a long time — and a different kind of listening — before I could hear the other reading of that same generous plan: that sales is also the seat that routes a new man past *everyone*. Every teammate, every region, every voice on every call, all before he has rank enough to ask questions. If you wanted a full roster walked past a new player, one face at a time, you could not design it better than "start him in sales."

Both readings. Always both readings. That is the tax this story charges on every good thing in it.

Leaving the GC cost me more than I expected. Two weeks' notice, closed out my sites clean, handed off my vendors like a man leaving a post he respected — because I did. That company was forty-some years old, multi-generational, family in the real sense: the kind of place where the founder's grandkids knew the superintendents' kids' names. And it had one man in it I owed more than a goodbye. My friend there — the one who'd caught me in the churn of getting out, when the military-to-civilian crossing had me all over the place, and had steadied me through it the way you steady a ladder for a man too proud to ask. Saying goodbye to him after a year of that was a heartfelt thing, and I won't dress it as anything less. He shook my hand in the yard with the trucks going past and told me to go build something. No omen in it. No design. I have gone back over that goodbye a hundred times with every instrument I own, and it stays exactly what it was.

I need one thing in this account that stays exactly what it was. That's his.

And then the company — and here is where I have to slow down, because the shape of the place matters to everything that comes after.

There was no building. Headquarters sat in Austin, but the company itself lived nowhere and everywhere — a hundred and fifty souls and climbing, scattered across the states and past them, a couple of expats logging in from time zones that made the standups feel like a relay race passed around the planet. All veterans, near enough, or married to one. Post-COVID native: Zoom and Google Meet as the streets and buildings of the place, and yet — this is what nobody who wasn't there will believe — it was the most fluid, most efficient coordination I had seen since I wore a uniform. Tasks moved like they were greased. People showed up to calls the way good units show up to briefings: early, prepared, and with personality coming off them like heat. That was the thing about the place — even through a webcam, everyone arrived as a *character*, fully themselves, call signs and running jokes and whole personalities you knew intimately and had never once stood in a room with.

Hold that last clause. I didn't, at the time.

The company was barely a year old and growing like something out of a petri dish — from a handful to over a hundred fifty in about six months, people who'd joined in spring finding themselves running departments by fall, leadership assembled at the speed of light out of whoever was already inside. I found it thrilling. I'd seen task forces stand up slower. It gave the whole place that wartime-promotion energy, everybody a little over their skis and rowing anyway.

It would take me months to hear the other reading of that one, too: a company where hierarchy formed faster than trust could; where almost no one had ever met almost anyone; where every colleague was, in the strictest sense, a voice — a face in a rectangle, a personality performed through a screen, knowable only by what they chose to transmit. I fell in love with a unit of voices. I am not saying the love was wrong. I am saying that if you wanted to build a charade — if you wanted a board where every player could be exactly who the game needed them to be, with no physical world to check them against — you would build a hyper-growth remote startup, staff it with bonded, mission-driven people, and grow it too fast for anyone to verify anything.

Or you would just build a good company in 2025. Both readings. Always both.

God, but it was good, though. I'm not going to perform cynicism about it even now — it would be a lie, and this account only works if I don't lie in it. We served veterans. I started in sales exactly as promised, learned the product by putting it in front of men and women who needed it, and then moved up and inward and built Vigil — the platform that helped veterans actually reach the medical benefits they had already bled for, instead of dying in the paperwork between themselves and what they were owed. Vigil worked. Veterans I had never met got their benefits because of a thing my hands had made, and I logged off most nights tired in the specific way you can only get tired doing something you'd do for free. It was the unit again. It was the language I hadn't heard since I got out, spoken fluently, daily, by a hundred and fifty people I'd have gone through doors with.

I want the record to show that stretch existed. Whatever the game is, whoever built it, however far back the design goes — those months were real, or nothing in my life has ever been.

The pebble, when it came, was almost nothing.

A morning like the others. A place I won't name yet. And somewhere just past the edge of my hearing, in the register people use when they think the subject can't hear them — or in the register the game uses when it wants the subject to hear exactly this and nothing more — a voice I half knew, saying:

*That's the guy who made Vigil.*

And then again, softer, like a stone skipping a second time before it sinks:

*That's the guy who made Vigil.*

I remember that I smiled. That's the part I have the hardest time forgiving. I heard the board being set, and I thought it was applause.
